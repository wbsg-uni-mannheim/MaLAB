MaLAB: Mannheim Legal Agent Benchmark

Evaluating German legal reasoning with LLM agents, case-specific rubrics, and web research

Abstract

Large language models have developed from chatbots into agents that plan their work, use tools, and produce complete documents. Private individuals and legal professionals increasingly use them to draft legal texts. MaLAB investigates how agent workflows and access to research tools affect the quality of these drafts under German law. Its tasks are exam cases from both German state examinations. Cases at the level of the First State Examination ask for a complete legal opinion in the prescribed appraisal style (Gutachtenstil). Cases at the level of the Second State Examination ask for the documents of legal practice, such as judgments, briefs and decisions of the public prosecutor. Each answer is scored against a rubric of atomic pass/fail criteria derived from the reference solution, so results can be traced to individual reasoning steps.

The public release contains 45 First State Examination cases with published reference solutions, 15 each in public, criminal and civil law, with 1,806 criteria in total. A private test set of 45 Second State Examination cases is held back. Rubrics are generated by LLMs, calibrated against the reference solution, tested with deliberately defective answers, and developed with feedback from legal professionals. Submissions are scored per criterion by a committee of two LLM judges with a third model as tiebreaker. Two lawyers have reviewed random samples of 400 criteria from both examinations and the corresponding judge verdicts.

We compare ten configurations: three models answering in a single call, the same models using OpenCode, Codex with and without web search, and GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5, each in the command-line agent built by its vendor. We examine where working through a task over several steps improves a solution, what web research contributes, and which legal requirements the answers satisfy.

Introduction

LLM agents used in legal work produce complete documents such as legal opinions, judgments and briefs. The result of a legal case says little on its own. In German legal education and practice, a solution is judged by the reasoning that leads to the result, and a correct result with missing or wrong reasoning does not pass. An evaluation of legal agents therefore has to check the individual reasoning steps of a complete document, not only its final result. For US practice, Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) follows this approach. It evaluates agents on more than 1,200 realistic drafting tasks scored against expert-written rubrics of atomic pass/fail criteria, building on the earlier BigLaw Bench. LAB is now publicly released. US practice differs from German law in doctrine, language, and document conventions.

For German law, BenGER scores complete solutions to exam cases on ten general rubric dimensions such as correctness, subsumption and legal method, and validates its LLM judges against human ratings. The models are evaluated in a single call, with retrieval and tool use disabled.

MaLAB evaluates how LLM agents solve German legal cases. Models run in agent harnesses such as Claude Code, Codex CLI and OpenCode, with and without web search, and are compared with the same models answering in a single call. The tasks cover both German state examinations: published cases from the First State Examination and a private set of cases from the Second State Examination, which asks for documents of legal practice such as judgments and briefs. Instead of general rubric dimensions, each case has its own pass/fail criteria for the individual legal requirements, derived from the reference solution. A judge applies these criteria without seeing the solution and records a rationale for each verdict, so that errors can be traced to specific reasoning steps. Lawyers reviewed samples of the criteria and labelled judge verdicts to validate the judge.

This page documents the current benchmark and ongoing research. The pipeline, tasks and rubrics, the answers and per-criterion verdicts of all ten systems and the documentation of the legal review are available on GitHub. Additional experiments are in progress. MaLAB starts with German law and is designed to grow into further European jurisdictions.

Tasks

Becoming a fully qualified legal professional in Germany, whether judge, public prosecutor or lawyer, requires passing two state examinations: the First State Examination (Erste Juristische Prüfung) at the end of university legal studies, and the Second State Examination after a two-year practical traineeship. MaLAB draws its tasks from both levels.

In the first examination, candidates solve a fictional case in Gutachtenstil, a rigid appraisal style that examines every claim or offence step by step, and hand in one long written legal opinion. The public release contains 45 cases on this level, 15 each in public law, criminal law and civil law. 43 cases come from the Zeitschrift für das Juristische Studium (ZJS), an open-access legal education journal, via the BenGER corpus, and two from the OpenRewi casebook on administrative law. Each case was published together with a reference solution by its academic authors. Attribution, source and licence are recorded per task.

Each task consists of an instruction, the case documents (the facts plus any annexes), and the name of the file in which the system must hand in its solution. The reference solution and the rubric are not visible to the system.

Second State Examination (private test set)

The Second State Examination concludes the two-year legal traineeship (Referendariat). Candidates no longer write a university-style legal opinion. They draft the document a judge, public prosecutor or lawyer would produce from a case file, and they examine questions the document leaves open in an auxiliary opinion (Hilfsgutachten).

The private test set contains 45 cases, 15 each in public law, civil law and criminal law, with 2,317 rubric criteria in total. The required work products include judgments and court decisions, briefs and client letters, oral case presentations (Aktenvortrag), final orders of the public prosecutor, appeal opinions and closing speeches. Many tasks require two or three documents. All public law cases are original exam cases of the Bavarian Second State Examination. The civil and criminal law cases are exam-preparation cases from German legal education journals and original exam cases of a state examination office.

Like Humanity's Last Exam, ARC-AGI and SWE-bench Pro, MaLAB keeps part of its tasks private. Some of these cases and their solutions are available elsewhere, but the rubrics and the graded answers are not. Keeping them private makes it harder to train or tune systems directly on the benchmark. We therefore do not publish the cases, the case list, the rubrics or the answers, and report only aggregate results. Rubrics follow the same pipeline as for the first examination, with document-specific criteria for form, structure and the auxiliary opinion.

Rubric-Based Judgment

A legal case solution can reach the correct result through flawed reasoning, or a defensible different result through sound reasoning. Comparing only the final result, or asking a model for one overall grade, hides where a solution succeeds and where it fails. MaLAB therefore breaks each reference solution into atomic pass/fail criteria, one for each examination step a correct solution has to take. A judge decides each criterion separately. This has several advantages:

  • Traceable scores. Every lost point names the step that is missing or wrong: the legal basis, the rule, the application to the facts, or an argument.
  • Missing reasoning becomes visible. Criteria ask for the result and its reason at the place where the question arises, so a bare conclusion does not pass.
  • Verdicts can be checked. Each verdict answers a narrow question and comes with quoted evidence from the answer, so a lawyer can check it in minutes.
  • Errors can be compared. Criteria carry their function in the solution, so failures can be grouped by type of step, area of law and system.

Related work. Rubrics of atomic criteria are an established approach for open-ended professional tasks. HealthBench scores medical conversations against criteria written by physicians. PRBench covers law and finance with 19,356 expert-written criteria for 1,100 tasks. LexRubric applies 12,337 expert-written criteria to Chinese legal consultation and judicial examination tasks. Harvey's LAB scores agents on drafting tasks of US legal practice against expert rubrics. LEXam grades open answers to Swiss law exams with an LLM judge against reference answers. For German law, BenGER scores complete case solutions on ten weighted rubric dimensions. The models evaluated there write their solutions in a single call, with retrieval and tool use disabled.

MaLAB differs in two respects. Its criteria are derived for each case from the published reference solution, and the judge applies them without seeing that solution. The solutions are produced by agents that plan, draft and revise over several steps, in part with web search, and compared with the same models answering in a single call.

Benchmark Pipeline

Each exam case is used in two ways. The reference solution is turned into a calibrated rubric of atomic pass/fail criteria, and the facts and instructions form the task that the evaluated system solves. A judge committee then scores the submission against the rubric, one criterion at a time.

Overview of the MaLAB pipeline. The judges receive the submission and the criterion text, never the reference solution. The following sections describe each stage.

Rubric Generation

Every case is scored against a rubric of atomic, binary pass/fail criteria derived from the published reference solution. A rubric contains several dozen criteria per case. A criterion states what counts as fulfilled and what does not, and it must stand on its own: the judge that later applies it never sees the reference solution. Where the official solution marks alternative legal paths as defensible, the criterion names the acceptable alternatives explicitly.

A submission therefore receives dozens of individual verdicts per case, and every failed criterion names the reasoning step it stands for. Each criterion consists of a fulfilled condition describing the required examination step, a not-fulfilled condition describing the disqualifying errors, and metadata used in analysis: the function of the step, a diagnostic criticality between one and three stars, and the position of the step in the expected line of analysis.

Rubrics are generated by an LLM pipeline (gpt-5.6-sol) in six steps:

1 Atomize

Split the reference solution into legally material statements: individual claims, definitions, subsumption steps, and results.

The reference solution of the example case “Zwei Autokäufe” yields 40 atoms, among them: a claim of F against G for the purchase price under § 433 Abs. 2 BGB requires a valid contract between F and G.

2 Generate

Three generator roles (doctrine, fact grounding, adversary) each draft candidate criteria, producing an intentionally over-complete pool.

For the example case, the three roles draft 90 candidate criteria, several of them competing variants for the same atom.

3 Prune

Merge the pool into a rubric of atomic Boolean criteria, removing duplicates and compound criteria that would make verdicts ambiguous.

The 90 candidates for the example case are merged into the 24 criteria of the final rubric.

4 Calibrate

A three-model committee judges the reference solution against every criterion. Criteria the gold solution does not pass unanimously are rewritten, split, or dropped, for at most two rounds.

In the example case, five criteria were revised after the first round and one after the second. The reference solution passes all 24.

5 Outline

Extract the solution's structure as a tree and anchor each criterion to its position in the expected line of analysis.

Criterion C-001 below is anchored to outline node I, the claim of F against G under § 433 Abs. 2 BGB.

6 Tag

Classify each criterion by function (structure, legal basis, rule statement, application, argumentation, conclusion, form) and by criticality (1–3 stars, diagnostic only).

C-001 below carries the function tag legal basis and criticality two of three stars.

Rubric Validation

A machine-generated rubric can fail in two directions. A rubric that is too lax awards criteria the submission does not earn, and a rubric that is too strict fails correct legal work, for example by insisting on one doctrinal path where the law allows several. Our validation workflow therefore includes positive calibration, adversarial checks, and legal review.

Positive checks. During generation, criteria are calibrated against the published reference solution and refined if it fails them. This checks consistency with the source used to create the rubric.

Adversarial checks. We test rubrics and judge with deliberately defective answers: a bare conclusion without reasoning, analysis attached to the wrong person or part of the case, or a planted legal error. These checks help identify overly permissive criteria and judge rules.

Feedback from legal professionals. Legal scholars have reviewed sample materials and judged answers. Their feedback informs revisions to criteria, evidence attribution, legal presentation and the treatment of auxiliary opinions.

Review of random samples. To measure how often rubric and judge are right, lawyers reviewed random samples of criteria. Each sampled criterion is shown with the answer of one randomly chosen system and the judge's verdict. The reviewer answers two questions: is the criterion legally correct, and is the verdict correct? For the first examination, a lawyer from the University of Mannheim reviewed 200 of the 1,777 content criteria, stratified by area of law and criterion weight. For the second examination, two lawyers each reviewed 100 criteria, one in public law and one in civil law, with the answers spread evenly over the ten systems.

Rubric and judge are right in most cases in both examinations. Most rubric problems are criteria that are too narrow or too case-specific, or that turn a side remark of the reference solution into a requirement. In public law most judge errors are too strict: the judge fails an answer whose explanations make clear that the requirement is met without stating it expressly. In civil law most judge errors are too lenient: the judge accepts a provision that is named but not applied, or a result that is asserted without reasons.

Both reviewers of the second examination also labelled a shared set of rows. Both gave a clear label on 51 of them (48 public law, 3 civil law). The two lawyers agree on the verdict in 46 of these rows (90.2%, κ 0.75), and in all 46 the judge agrees with them. On the five rows where the lawyers disagree, the judge necessarily sides with one of them: four times with the civil law reviewer and once with the public law reviewer. Agreement between judge and lawyer is therefore at the level of agreement between two lawyers. As the reviewers saw the judge's verdict, this comparison likely favours the judge.

Failure-focused audit. Criteria with persistent failures or disputed votes are reviewed against the task and reference solution (see the error analysis below). Model-assisted review identifies candidates for legal assessment and rubric refinement.

Automated Judging

Each criterion is judged individually. The judge receives the task instructions, the submission, excerpts of the case documents the criterion refers to, and the criterion text. It never sees the reference solution. The judge must first quote verbatim evidence from the submission, then reason about it, and only then return a pass or fail verdict.

The judge first locates the passage where the answer examines the question of the criterion: the same person, claim or offence, the same provision and the same examination station. It decides on that passage only. A requirement counts as met when the answer states it expressly or when the explanations on this question make it clear. Where a criterion asks for a reason, a correct result at the correct place with a shorter, tenable reason suffices. These rules follow the feedback of the legal review.

For criteria tagged as application or argumentation, the same judge call also returns a separate verdict on legal method and presentation. It examines the legal premise, application to the facts, and reasoning leading to the result, using the style required by the work product. A substantively wrong argument can still be methodically complete.

Our judge committee uses models from three providers, so no model family holds the majority. GPT-6 Luna from OpenAI and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash assess each criterion first. If they agree, their verdict is accepted. If they disagree, Google’s Gemini 3.8 Flash casts the deciding vote. All three run with medium reasoning effort.

Failed judge calls are retried twice. Decisions that still lack the required votes are marked as unresolved and count as failures.

The content score of a run is the share of criteria passed, averaged case by case. The style verdicts aggregate into a separate style score over the application and argumentation criteria.

How one criterion is judged. The process repeats for every criterion of the rubric.

The complete judge prompt is part of the repository and can be inspected below. Placeholders in curly braces are filled per criterion at judging time.

Show the judge prompt (content and style verdict per criterion)

Judge prompt: prompts/evaluation/combined_content_style_criterion.v4.txt. Earlier prompt variants are available in the repository. Display punctuation is adapted for readability. Other criteria use a content-only prompt.

You are evaluating one Boolean legal-rubric criterion. Return two independent verdicts
from the same answer and context:

1. `style`: whether the attempted application or argumentation uses functional legal
   reasoning style;
2. `content`: whether the criterion is substantively fulfilled.

The criterion is tagged as application or argumentation, so both verdicts are required.
They must not be copied from one another. A substantively wrong argument may be
methodically complete (`content: fail`, `style: pass`), while a correct proposition may
be merely asserted (`content: pass`, `style: fail`).

The criterion may be written in German, English, French, or another case language.
Apply it in that language and do not translate legal terms unless needed for understanding.

Decide the style verdict first and record its method checks before deciding content.
Do not revise the style verdict merely because the later content verdict passes or fails.

## Style verdict
- Do not let the content verdict determine the style verdict. The legal rule, application,
  or conclusion may be substantively wrong and still be methodically well formed.
- The same person, legal object, issue, and examination station are hard requirements.
  Do not assemble a missing examination from statements scattered elsewhere.
- Pass only when the answer itself connects (1) a criterion-specific legal standard or
  decisive premise, (2) concrete facts or competing arguments, and (3) a conclusion that
  completes this exact line of examination.
- Routine points may be concise, but rule, fact, and result merely appearing near one
  another is insufficient without an expressed inferential connection.
- A standard for a neighboring issue is not criterion-specific. A complicity standard
  does not by itself examine immediate commencement, and distinguishing completed from
  uncompleted attempt does not by itself examine whether the attempt failed.
- A later voluntariness discussion that implies continued ability does not constitute a
  methodical examination of whether an attempt failed unless the answer itself identifies
  that non-failure question, applies its standard, and reaches its conclusion there or by
  an explicit, unambiguous cross-reference.
- Do not supply a missing premise, causal link, comparison, or conclusion. Irrelevant or
  misplaced exposition does not repair a missing application.
- A bare conclusion, definition without application, list of facts, or incomplete chosen
  examination path fails. Equivalent terminology and valid alternative structure pass.

## Content verdict
- The criterion text is the only substantive standard. Apply its PASS and FAIL conditions
  and do not add requirements it does not state.
- Work in two steps. First locate the passage where the answer examines the criterion's
  own question: the same person, object, claim or offence, the same provision or
  fundamental right, and the same examination station. Then decide on that passage.
- Take the result and its reasons from that passage. Do not combine a result stated in
  one place with reasons given elsewhere for a different provision, right, claim, person,
  or station, even if the same doctrinal term appears there (the same term, such as an aim, an intent, or a duty, discussed for another right, offence, or claim). If the
  answer does not examine the criterion's question anywhere, the criterion is not met;
  passages on other questions neither prove nor disprove it.
- Accept equivalent legal wording, synonyms, and a valid alternative structure unless the
  criterion requires a particular form or location. Exact wording rarely matters.
- A PASS component is met when the answer states it expressly or when the answer's
  explanations on this question make it clear, for example because the answer establishes
  something stronger that necessarily includes it (an actual effect that the answer establishes includes the mere possibility of that effect). It is not met when the answer never takes a step the criterion
  requires, such as a prerequisite or the exclusion of an alternative, even if the
  answer's result is consistent with that step.
- A FAIL condition describing a legal position applies only when the answer adopts that
  position as its rule.
- A named fact-pattern variant, hypothetical, or subpart (for example, the base case
  versus a modification) is part of scope. If the answer expressly assigns its reasoning
  to a different named variant than the criterion, set `same_scope` to false and return
  content `fail`, unless the answer explicitly and unambiguously cross-applies that
  reasoning to the required variant. Do not fail solely because the answer omits the
  variant label when its surrounding context still unambiguously addresses the required
  variant.
- Facts or intent discussed for one offence or station do not establish the subjective
  element of a distinct preparatory offence or other station unless the answer expressly
  assigns them there or makes an unambiguous cross-reference.
- A heading, issue statement, bare result, or nearby mention does not prove that a required
  rule, application, or argument was performed.
- Evaluate the reason actually given. A materially wrong provision, doctrine, station,
  person, object, or invented material fact fails when incompatible with the criterion.
- When the criterion asks for a reason ("weil", "because"), a correct result at the
  correct place with a tenable, shorter reason suffices; it need not repeat the
  criterion's reason. Naming only the result or the statutory label is not a reason.
- Split the PASS side into mandatory components. Return pass when each is met expressly
  or clearly from the answer's explanations and no FAIL condition applies. Return fail
  when a component is missing, materially wrong, examined only under a different
  question, or only possibly intended.

Evaluating your own submissions

The rubrics can score any answer to a benchmark case, including answers produced outside our harness. The repository contains the complete evaluation code. After cloning it and configuring API access, a single command judges an answer file against the rubric of a task: 

env/bin/python -m evaluation.run <task-dir> <answer-file> --judge-committee configs/judge-committee-luna6-deepseek41-gemini38-tiebreaker.json --committee-tiebreaker --style-evaluation --judge-prompt-version v4

The command reports the verdict, the quoted evidence, and the reasoning for every criterion, together with the aggregated content and style scores. The repository README documents the configuration options.

Results

We evaluate ten configurations in four harnesses:

  • Single call: the model receives the instructions and all case documents in one prompt and answers in one pass, without tools (gpt-5.6-sol, gpt-5.6-terra, deepseek-v4-pro).
  • OpenCode: the same three models work in the open-source OpenCode agent with file tools in a sandboxed workspace and can draft and revise their answer over several steps.
  • Codex CLI: OpenAI's command-line agent, with gpt-5.6-sol once without and once with web search, and with GPT-6 Astra without web search. In the web configuration, the source journal and known copies of the case solutions are blocked.
  • Claude Code: Anthropic's command-line agent with Claude Opus 5.5, without web search.

GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 run with high reasoning effort in the agent their vendor built for them, with shell access and external tools disabled. Apart from the web configuration, no system may use external sources.

Argumentation is the weakest step for all ten systems. Even Claude Opus 5.5 passes only 75 percent of the argumentation criteria, against 86 percent of the application criteria. The weakest systems pass between a quarter and a third. Argumentation criteria ask the answer to recognize a disputed question and to decide it with reasons.

What goes wrong in argumentation. A detailed reading of the argumentation failures in one civil law case (“Die falsche Gisela”) shows five patterns: the disputed question is not raised at all (33 percent), the weighing is incomplete (25 percent), the answer reaches a conclusion the reference solution does not support (19 percent), the expected result is given without the required reason (18 percent), or the right argument is made at the wrong place (4 percent).

Findings from the legal review

The lawyers who reviewed the random samples also commented on the answers. The cards below describe the problems they found, how often the systems fail the criteria concerned, and the reviewers' own words. Counts refer to the ten systems on the first examination.

Required questions are not examined

Problem. Answers solve the main question and skip further claims or legal questions the case raises, although the instructions ask to address all of them. A skipped question costs every criterion that belongs to it.

Examples. In a civil law case on unjust enrichment, the reviewed answer never examines the claim the criterion asks about: whether handing over a machine was a performance (Leistung) in the sense of § 812 BGB. 8 of 10 systems fail this criterion. In another case, all 10 systems fail the criterion on the aggravated liability under §§ 818 Abs. 4, 819 Abs. 1 BGB, and the reviewed answer does not address it at all. In a public law case, 8 of 10 systems fail the criterion on the plaintiff's capacity to sue, which the facts call into question.

Reviewer comment (first examination)

„Der eigentlich in Frage stehende Anspruch wird gar nicht geprüft.“

The claim actually at issue is not examined at all.

Reviewer comment (first examination)

„Die Prozessfähigkeit wird als solche gar nicht problematisiert.“

The capacity to sue is not discussed as an issue at all.

Results are asserted, not derived

Problem. Answers state the correct result or name the correct provision, but do not derive the result from the facts. In an exam, such a result earns no credit. Judges sometimes accept it: in the civil law sample of the second examination, the judge was too lenient in 6 of 100 rows, mostly in such cases.

Examples. An answer names the correct amount of 300 € without any reasoning. In the second examination, an answer by Claude Opus 5.5 states a correct result without factual reasons, and another answer only names a provision without applying it.

Reviewer comment (first examination)

„Es wird zwar zutreffend von 300 € ausgegangen, aber ohne jegliche Begründung.“

The answer correctly starts from 300 €, but without any reasoning.

Reviewer comment (second examination)

„Es fehlt eine sachliche Begründung für die (richtige!) Behauptung der KI. In einer Klausur würde man das als Zufallstreffer bewerten.“

There is no substantive reasoning for the AI's (correct!) assertion. In an exam, this would be graded as a lucky guess.

Reviewer comment (second examination)

„Die Ausführung geht aber über bloßes Name-Dropping nicht hinaus.“

The discussion does not go beyond mere name-dropping.

Problems are noticed but not worked out

Problem. Answers recognize where a case is difficult but stop before the decisive step. A reviewer observed this pattern in the answers of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5: they identify the problems of a case but do not examine them in full. A lawyer working with such a system could take over at exactly this point.

Example. In a constitutional law case, answers affirm that the applicant is personally affected without discussing the age limit of the statute, which is the actual problem at this point. 3 of 10 systems fail this criterion.

Reviewer comment (first examination)

„An der maßgeblichen Stelle in der Zulässigkeit wird die Selbstbetroffenheit der J bejaht, ohne vertieft auf die Altersproblematik einzugehen.“

At the relevant point in the admissibility check, the answer affirms that J is personally affected without going into the age problem.

Legal style separates the systems

Problem. A legal document has to follow the form of its type: the appraisal style of an opinion, the structure of a judgment, the conventions of a brief. In the legal reading, the presentation mattered more than expected.

Observation. Answers of the single-call models are often hard to use as legal work products. The answers of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 are close to the expected form. The style scores in the results show the same order.

Findings from the model-assisted review

An earlier exploratory model-assisted review selected 218 criteria that no system passed with certainty. The following examples come from this review and were passed on to the legal review.

Side issues are dropped

The exploratory review flags omissions: answers solve the main claim and skip secondary claim bases such as unjust enrichment, tort, or possession claims, although many cases explicitly instruct candidates to address all raised questions, if necessary in an auxiliary opinion.

One wrong turn costs many criteria

An early doctrinal error can affect several criteria. The model review flags possible examples for legal assessment. In the civil law case “Ehe und Espresso”, reviewed answers award the spouses co-ownership of a household purchase that the prevailing view denies, and five criteria hang on that turn. In the criminal law case “Grillen mit Hindernissen”, reviewed answers apply § 315 StGB directly and skip § 315e StGB, which costs five criteria at once.

Facts are misread

Systems overlook decisive details of the facts. In the criminal law case “Die Reise ins Gericht”, reviewed answers misread the perpetrator's mental image of a witness and produce four follow-up errors from that one misreading. In the commercial law case “Bezahlen für den Porsche der anderen?”, reviewed answers miss that the decisive transaction predates the register entry, the fact that carries the accepted teleological reduction of § 15 Abs. 1 HGB.

The wrong legal standard is applied

Systems reach a defensible-looking result through the wrong test. In the state liability case “Räumungsanspruch und Mietausfallschaden”, the systems deny negligence because the officials lacked actual knowledge, missing the objective standard of § 276 BGB, and three criteria fall with it. In the money laundering case “Finder-Schwindler”, the systems affirm the predicate offences but overlook the additional requirements that the current wording of § 261 Abs. 7 StGB attaches to self-laundering.

These patterns motivate questions about omitted issues, the treatment of follow-on errors, and the connection between case facts and legal conclusions. We are examining these patterns with the final verdicts and input from legal scholars.

The review also identified potential problems in our own evaluation: overly strict criteria, valid alternative paths not covered by the rubric, and evidence attributed to the wrong part of an answer. Candidate corrections and contested decisions are documented for follow-up. Some rubric edits still await re-scoring.

Outlook

Our next steps are the following:

  • Systems as lawyers use them. We will evaluate the vendor agents with web search enabled, as they are delivered to users.
  • Rubric weights. Following the legal review, we will revise how criteria are weighted that only check whether facts from the case file are restated correctly.
  • Comparison with human grading. Legal examiners will grade selected answers as they would grade an exam, so that rubric scores can be compared with grades.

We welcome legal scholars, practitioners and AI researchers interested in reviewing criteria, alternative solutions, or agent and web-search experiments. Please contact Aaron Steiner or open an issue in the GitHub repository.
